My research is at the intersection between vision and cognitive science. With a background in visual perception and psychophysics, I study how humans process sensory information to make sense of the world around them. However, this "making sense of" is not purely a passive, bottom-up driven, perceptual process - it goes hand in hand with top-down inference processes that require understanding the human mind as a more hollistic system that is engaging in behaviourally relevant tasks in a complex world. My research thus connects a rigorous experimental approach with the goal to make statements about natural human cognition and behaviour in the real world.

Human scene understanding
======
We can usually make sense of the complex visual world around us within a fraction of a second. How do we achieve this remarkable feat? Which information do we use to categorize scenes as kitchens and classrooms? My research investigates human scene understanding using behavioural experiments and psychophysics, eye-tracking, and computational modelling. 
  
In a series of experiments, we could show that global scene information has a limited utility for human scene categorization (Wiesmann & Võ, [2022](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/xhp0001020), [2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0001342)), whereas single objects are often sufficient for scene categorization if they are presented close to the fixation point ([Wiesmann & Võ, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-32385-y)). However, we seem to use information flexibly depending on its availability ([Wiesmann et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-93252-2); [Wiesmann & Võ, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0001342)).

What makes an object diagnostic?
======
Based on the observation that seeing single objects can often be enough to categorize ("diagnose") a scene, we systematically assessed which properties make an object diagnostic ([Wiesmann & Võ, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-32385-y)). We could show that object specificity (how often an object occurs in scenes of a category relative to scenes of other categories) and object frequency (how many scenes of a category contain the object) were the best predictors of an object's ability to enable scene categorization.

Measuring object frequency and specificity
======
To investigate visual processing with high ecological validity, we need tools that adequately represent the world as we see it in our everyday lives. But how can we reliably estimate how frequent different objects are in our environment (e.g., airfryers or rice cookers in kitchens)?
  
Typically, vision scientists use large databases of real-world scene images to estimate object frequency. However, in a recent [project](/talks), we showed that these databases massively underrepresent typical everyday objects such as toothpaste, sponges, or cutlery. We therefore argue that databases should be used with caution and warn against the "panoptic fallacy" - the assumption that databases and AI models trained on them see the world the way we do.

Understanding opportunities for action in scenes
======
One of the primary purposes of perception is to prepare and guide actions. How do we know that we can cook, sleep, or work in an environment? In recent work carried out with [Lea Müller Karoza](https://www.lmu.de/psyedu/de/personen/kontaktseite/lea-mueller-karoza-55d11cf2.html), we showed that anchor objects, usually large and stationary objects such as a sink that predict the presence and location of surrounding objects such as soap and towel, also carry relevant action information and give rise to the action opportunities one perceives in a scene (Müller Karoza, Wiesmann & Võ, [2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-04122-0), [2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/17470218261480626)).

All things bias  
======
I am deeply fascinated by the many errors our cognitive system produces. This includes classic demonstrations of inattentional blindness, visual illusions, memory biases and reasoning biases but also their application and relevance for our everyday life (e.g., how cognitive biases and beliefs shape each other).
